# Writing within an approved proposal: deepen the research plan without changing its framework

English translation by OC Science Lab, 2026-09-09. Original licensing and attribution apply.

Use when a research plan sits within an already approved project: the proposal has been funded, its premise must remain intact, and the implementation can be developed in greater depth.

## Triggers

- The user says the proposal has been approved, or that someone else applied and their research plan must follow it.
- The complete proposal is sensitive and cannot be shared; the user can provide only excerpts.
- The proposal fixes the section structure, research components and division of responsibilities between the user's subproject and other subprojects.
- The user says not to introduce a new direction or change the premise.

## Differences from compose mode

| Dimension | Standard compose | Writing within an approved proposal |
|---|---|---|
| Research question | Derived from a literature gap | Already specified; deepen it without redefining it |
| Research structure | Freely designed | Fill the fixed components of the approved framework |
| Innovation | Argued independently | Integrate naturally into existing work rather than claim a separate agenda |
| Scientific tension | Build a narrative arc | Deepen the tension already established in the proposal |
| References | Open literature search | Existing proposal references plus the user's own review |
| Scope | Defined by the user | Defined by the proposal; confirm any extension |

## Receiving information

Proposal material often arrives as pasted excerpts from separate sections:

1. **Collect the material before analysis.** Confirm receipt of the abstract, relevant research background, research contents, objectives, key scientific questions, methods, technical route, innovations and expected outcomes before planning.
2. **Record each source location:** abstract → §0; research contents → §3.2.3; methods → §4.1.4, for example, so later references remain traceable.
3. **Identify responsibility boundaries:** distinguish other work packages from the current author's work to avoid scope overreach.
4. **Extract hard constraints:** performance targets, prohibited wording, material or operating ranges, and formatting requirements.

## Writing strategy

### 1. Develop the existing framework instead of starting another project

- Match the proposal's research-content numbering, such as “3.2.3 (1) Key variables”.
- Use the terminology already defined in the proposal; do not rename or regroup it unilaterally.
- Use “[n]” for existing proposal references, retaining its reference numbering.
- Do not try to surpass the proposal at the level of its scientific question: that question belongs to the team; the current work specifies its third subproject.

### 2. Add detail at the implementation level

Proposals often describe methods broadly (“use methods including … to investigate …”). Develop:

- **Experimental matrices:** specific gradients, replicate counts, temperatures and time points.
- **Method details:** electrode materials, potential parameters, atmosphere compositions and combinations of characterization methods.
- **Decision criteria:** what counts as success or failure, and what follows failure.
- **Independent execution:** avoid dependence on data that other subprojects have not yet produced.

### 3. Integrate your additional methods naturally

If you have a method absent from the proposal, do not create a separate chapter just to showcase novelty. Instead:

- Include it as a candidate method for an existing research task.
- Use it to evaluate effects or monitor states.
- Present it as part of a natural method combination in the technical route.
- Write “following existing methods, explore applicability in the target system” rather than “this study pioneers”.

### 4. Specify independent execution

For a proposal with parallel workstreams, explain:

- How input samples or data can be obtained independently of unfinished work packages.
- Where key model parameters and boundary conditions come from.
- Which platform supports the experiments or evaluation.
- How later results from other work packages will provide cross-validation, rather than becoming prerequisites.

## Foundation files

- Give `01_research_canon.md` two source categories: literature facts and a separate **proposal constraints** section.
- In `02_evidence_table.md`, distinguish claims from the proposal, the user's own review, and inference.
- Base the scientific tension in `03_argument_map.md` on the proposal's existing gap; do not invent another.
- Keep research-content numbering in `04_section_contracts.md` consistent with the proposal.
- In `05_style_guide.md`, emphasize no separate agenda and no unsupported first-of-its-kind claims.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Treating the proposal as a literature review:** it is a funding application, not a published article. Cite it as “(proposal §X.X)”, not as published evidence.
2. **Crossing work-package boundaries:** do not design another lead's experiments or promise their deliverables.
3. **Turning hypotheses into conclusions:** a route marked “to be verified” remains a hypothesis in the research plan; do not upgrade it to “will establish”.
4. **Omitting fallback routes:** exploratory methods, such as electrochemical purification, require a fallback if they fail.
5. **Equating a doctoral researcher with the project lead:** publication, patent and training quotas may be project-wide targets, unsuitable as individual scientific deliverables. Prefer datasets, methods, process plans and decision criteria.
6. **Keeping infeasible routes central:** remove or explicitly downgrade a route when evidence shows it is infeasible. Whether to explain the failure in the main text depends on the document's purpose; extra wording must not disguise a rejected route as a deliverable workstream.
