# Chinese Academic Review Writing Style

English translation by OC Science Lab, 2026-09-09. Original licensing and attribution apply.

This file defines general style constraints for writing reviews in Chinese. Compared with proposals, reviews require greater factual density, clearer evidence levels and more restrained academic language. The guidance is translated here; it still concerns Chinese-language output when requested.

## Titles and headings

Headings are structural signposts, not narrative rhetoric. State the research object and analytical perspective. Avoid metaphor, personification, slogans and conversational questions.

| Avoid | Reason | Suggested revision |
|---|---|---|
| “What determines a material's fate” | Personification | “Factors affecting material stability” |
| “Parameter X: the master switch for performance” | Exaggerated metaphor | “How parameter X controls performance” |
| “How to see interface changes” | Conversational question | “Methods for characterizing interface changes” |
| “Engineering gains and chemical costs” | Narrative rhetoric | “Dual effects of treatment conditions” |

## Paragraph structure

- Give each paragraph one identifiable argument.
- Put the argument first, followed by evidence, comparisons and limitations.
- Short paragraphs of 3–5 sentences usually work better than long paragraphs with several arguments.
- Make transitions through logical relationships, not filler such as “furthermore”, “on the other hand” or “it is worth noting”.
- If Chinese body text needs first-line indentation, set it consistently in the export specification, excluding headings, captions and references.

## Expressions to avoid or use cautiously

| Expression | Risk | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| With the development of … | Generic, low-information opening | State the relevant background fact directly |
| It is worth noting | Substitutes a value judgment for evidence | Delete it or demonstrate importance with results |
| Undoubtedly; as everyone knows | Absolute and unsourced | Delete, qualify or cite |
| Is of great significance | Unspecified contribution | Explain the change to understanding, methods or decisions |
| Fills a gap | May overstate novelty | Qualify using a verifiable search finding |
| Systematic; comprehensive; in-depth | Often empty modifiers | State coverage or analytical dimensions |
| First; second; third; finally | Can create mechanical scaffolding | Retain only for a real sequence |

## Scientific language

- **Give numbers and conditions, not adjectives alone.** Replace unsupported “significantly improved” with “increased by 28.6%”; replace “high concentration” with “≥0.10 wt%”.
- **Distinguish evidence levels.** “Experiments show”, “the model predicts”, “the authors hypothesize” and “remains to be verified” are not interchangeable.
- **Choose attribution verbs precisely.** Use “report” for results, “propose” for ideas or models, and “demonstrate” only when the evidence excludes the main alternative explanations.
- **Preserve applicability limits.** State the materials, conditions, timescales or measurement methods for numbers, mechanisms and comparisons.
- **Standardize notation.** Keep subscripts, superscripts, units, italic variables and reversible/irreversible reaction symbols consistent.

## Common signs of formulaic AI prose

1. Mechanically restating the previous sentence with “the above analysis shows” or “in summary”.
2. Adding “systematic investigation”, “in-depth discussion” or “comprehensive analysis” without defining coverage.
3. Repeatedly using “not only … but also …” without a real progression.
4. Ending paragraphs with stock phrases such as “provides a reference for future research” or “offers engineering guidance”.
5. Adding excessive connectors to create fluency that conceals gaps in the evidence chain.
6. Giving every paragraph the same length, syntax and ending, creating a templated rhythm.

Restore the argument, evidence and limitations before editing sentence patterns. Linguistic variation cannot conceal a scientific problem.

## Sentence preferences

- Make subjects explicit; reduce potentially ambiguous pronouns equivalent to “its”, “that” and “this”.
- Put one main judgment in each sentence; consider splitting Chinese sentences longer than approximately 40 characters.
- Active phrasing is usually clearer than translated passive constructions: “experiments show” is better than “is shown by experiments”.
- Make causal, contrasting, comparative or limiting relationships explicit between adjacent sentences; do not pursue superficial smoothness.

## Literature handling

- Give every key factual statement in the first draft a traceable author–year reference, number or DOI placeholder.
- When several papers support the same fact, prioritize original, representative and methodologically comparable sources rather than piling up citations.
- Do not treat a review author's paraphrase as primary evidence; trace important claims to the original research.
- Do not supply causal explanations or conclusions that cited authors did not explicitly advance.
- For conflicting findings, compare conditions, methods and uncertainty rather than forcing a single conclusion.
